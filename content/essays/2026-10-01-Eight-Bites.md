## I did not understand _Eight Bites_ when I first read it
Succinctly: A woman gets gastric bypass surgery, like her sisters did, and loses a great deal of weight. Then the lost weight takes the form of a golem, which she beats to death; however the golem doesn't die, and instead follows her around, passively helping in small ways but never making itself seen, until the woman dies.

After my first read, I got the obvious things: 
* The woman's sisters are flat reflections of single emotions like vanity and shame, and are as two-dimensional and paper thin as their post-surgery bodies
* Shedding weight also caused our main character to shed her connection with her daughter
* The joy and conquest from excising all the unwanted parts of your body and killing them, bloodily and violently, with your own hands. 

But after all the grief and violence came love, and that vexed me.

## How lucky she was that the flesh golem chose love

### Back to the story
Each of those possible endings has as precise and true moral: fighting yourself is a losing battle, you cannot run from yourself, and relentless pursuit of a goal makes you lose sight of yourself. So why did Machado eschew them in favour of a moral about loving yourself?

I think it is because the author is not trying to scare readers with a cautionary tale, but show them that love is the only winning play.

## she could have chosen love from the start and saved herself a lot of trouble
> "Arms will lift me from my bed—her arms. They will be mother-soft, like dough and moss. I will recognize the smell. I will flood with grief and shame... I will cry as she shuffles me away from myself, toward a door propped open into the salty morning. I will curl into her body, which was my body once, but I was a poor caretaker and she was removed from my charge." -- 
[Carmen Maria Machado, _Eight Bites_](https://gulfcoastmag.org/stories/eight-bites,716){.margin domain="gulfcoastmag.org" title="Eight Bites - Gulf Coast Literary Journal" desc="As they put me to sleep, my mouth fills with the dust of the moon. I expect to choke on the silt but instead it slides in and out, and in and out, and I am, impossibly, breathing. I have dreamt &hellip;"}

All of my revised endings revolve around conflict. After all, every good story needs conflict! Yet Machado subverts that when her flesh golem refuses to haunt the woman, refuses to chase her, refuses to change her further. Instead the "monster" acquiesces, respects the woman's boundaries, loves from a distance, and forgives. Conflicts are neatly wrapped up with a winner and a loser, so Machado invites reflection instead.

And the truth is that the story ends about the same whether the woman got bypass surgery or not: she dies, aged 79. But in this story she dies hungry and unsure if it was worth it. And when she learns the truth she is flooded with grief and shame. It is sad to see that she only experienced self-love after passing.

My take is that the bypass surgery didn't cut away the woman's stomach, nor did it help her shed her fat. Instead, it cut away her empathy for herself, and took away her capacity to love beyond what she saw. I think her grief at the end came from realizing that she had lost much more than excess fat. I think she could have chosen to love herself, as she was, from the beginning of the story and saved herself a lot of trouble.

And if I apply that to myself, I can understand my own vexation. By relating so hard to the story, my own grief and shame surfaces. I am forced to consider my own advice and ponder the difficulty of simply "choosing love." It would be much easier to choose a surgery that could make me wealthy, or fix me up with the perfect partner, or assuage my anxiety forever. But such things would also render me static and frozen at perfection, inhuman and unable to change. And the story says that those feelings would catch up with me anyway, at the end. The only winning play, then, is not to run, fight, or hide, but to love yourself.
